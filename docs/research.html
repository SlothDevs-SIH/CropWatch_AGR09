Research: why we are building Crop Watch

Problem statement AGR-09: village-scale crop stress early warning from satellite imagery. This page explains what is happening in Maharashtra now, why a tool like this is needed, what already exists, and what we learned by building and testing it. Last updated 4 November 2026.

1. What is happening right now

The 2026 Kharif drought in Marathwada

  • The 2026 monsoon brought a widespread rain deficit across Maharashtra, hardest in Marathwada, where Jalna lies. The state formally declared drought in late September, with an order on 25 September 2026 (as reported), and reporting put the declared area at 265 talukas (as reported).
  • Soybean, the main Kharif crop in Marathwada, was reported to have lost roughly one third of its production (as reported), with pest and disease pressure rising on drought-stressed crops, for example in Nanded (as reported).
  • Rainfall in the pilot district: June to October 2026 was about 52% of the 2018 to 2025 average (our measurement, NASA POWER, a coarse grid). This agrees with the roughly 46% deficit reported.

It is not a one-off

The year before, 2025, the same state faced severe monsoon floods and large crop damage with a big relief response (as reported). Marathwada swings between flood and drought, so a tool built only for one extreme would not be enough.

The human cost

Marathwada has a long record of farm distress. Reporting counts 5,075 farmer suicide deaths between 2021 and 2025, and a sharp rise during 2025 (as reported). We mention this because it is why speed and fairness of relief matter, and we handle the subject with care: Crop Watch does not claim to prevent it.

  • Declaration is slow and coarse. Drought declaration follows a formal multi-indicator process (rainfall, crop condition, moisture) and then central verification. The national disaster rules also use a fixed gate date (as reported, early October for Kharif), and verification can lag behind what farmers already see in the field.
  • Insurance payouts depend on slow, manual measurement. Under the national crop insurance scheme (PMFBY), payouts rely largely on crop-cutting experiments and yield estimates, which have been widely reported as a bottleneck causing delays and disputes over claims (as reported).
  • Advice does not reach farmers fast. Technical advice reaches only part of farm households and mostly through general channels such as radio, TV and universities, not plot-specific alerts (as reported, national household survey).
  • Officers cannot visit every village. A taluka agriculture officer has far more villages than visiting days. A ranked "visit first" list is useful even if it only says where the crop looks worst this week.

So the need is not "another map". It is a cheap, repeatable, village-scale comparison with a normal season that officers can use to decide where to send people first, and that farmers can show as supporting evidence.

The core measure is NDVI, a standard index of green cover, from the free Sentinel-2 satellites (see the Data page). This is well-established science. The new work is applying it at village scale, aligning for crop age, and testing it honestly.

What this supports. In all eight whole talukas, 2026 sits above every ordinary season at the order date (Jafrabad only marginally). Our rule also beats a simple "less green than last reading" rule, which cannot see this drought at all. Thresholds were tuned without 2026 and then frozen.
What this does not support.
  • No early warning. The signal first stood out 4 to 14 days after the order (and not at all before it in Bhokardan and Jafrabad). We can show where, at about the time of the order, not earlier. An earlier statement of "+16 days lead" was an artefact of a badly placed sample box and was withdrawn.
  • Nothing is confirmed on the ground. No plot has been visited. A sealed, blind 40-plot field sample and the field kit are ready; the visits are the single most important next step.
  • A 4 km box misleads in both directions, so we moved to whole talukas.

6. What already exists, and what we borrowed

  • T.R.A.C.E. and our earlier GeoAI change-detection project taught us the landing-then-console flow, attention-based lists, a before/after swipe and an offline field workflow. We took ideas only. We wrote our own code, because T.R.A.C.E. has no licence file. See docs/TRACE_NOTES.md.
  • Government and commercial crop-monitoring programmes also use satellites. We have not audited them in detail, so we make no claim to be first. Our contribution is a plot-level, open-data, fully testable comparison with an honest accuracy page and a field-verification plan.

7. The pitch, kept honest

  1. Problem: loss is found late, coarsely and on paper, while farmers and officers need village-level information fast.
  2. Solution: a map and ranked list that show where this season differs from normal, plot by plot, with real before/after satellite pictures.
  3. Proof: eight whole talukas tested against seven past seasons, thresholds frozen, simple baseline beaten, results published with limits.
  4. Limits stated up front: after-the-order timing, no ground truth yet, no cause, no yield.
  5. Business idea: an evidence pack a farmer can attach to a claim, sold through service centres, simulated in the demo and subject to checking the real fee rules.

8. Open questions and next steps

  • Visit the sealed sample plots and measure how many alerts are confirmed and how many real problems we missed.
  • Test whether adding radar (cloud-proof) or rainfall earlier in the season can recover lead time.
  • Confirm the real form, fee and permission rules before any money feature is described as real.
  • Test on a real phone, with the network off, and have a native Marathi speaker review the text.
