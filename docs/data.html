Data: what we collected, where it came from, what it means

Crop Watch compares every farmland plot in Jalna district with what is normal for that time of year, using free satellite pictures. This page explains each dataset: its source, its contents, its technical meaning, and its limits. Last updated 4 November 2026.

In one line. Everything is free and open. Greenness comes from Sentinel-2, rainfall from NASA POWER, the farmland mask from ESA WorldCover, and taluka outlines from geoBoundaries. We invented none of it and bought none of it. We only measured and compared.

2. Satellite greenness (the main dataset)

What a satellite picture contains

Sentinel-2 photographs the ground roughly every five days (two satellites) in 13 colour bands. Each pixel on the bands we use is 10 m by 10 m. We use the red band (B04) and the near-infrared band (B08), plus a scene classification layer (SCL) that labels each pixel as cloud, cloud shadow, water, snow, vegetation, and so on.

What NDVI technically means

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red). Healthy leaves absorb red light for photosynthesis and strongly reflect near-infrared, so a dense green crop gives a high NDVI (about 0.6 to 0.9). Bare soil sits near 0.1 to 0.2. A crop that is drying, thinning, or growing late shows a lower NDVI than normal for that time of year. NDVI is a measure of how much green cover there is. It does not say why.

How a raw scene becomes a number

  1. Fetch only the window we need, never a full tile, for 2019 to 2026 (June to October each year).
  2. Mask cloud, cloud shadow, water and snow using the SCL layer.
  3. Aggregate into cells. A cell is a square of ground: 200 m in the original sample boxes (read at 20 m, so 100 pixels per cell), and 300 m for whole talukas (read at 60 m). A cell needs at least 30 clear pixels in a scene, otherwise it has no reading for that scene. We never fill the gap with a guess.
  4. Bin by time: readings are averaged into 10-day bins from 1 June (16 bins per season) so one cloudy week does not leave a hole.
  5. Keep only farmland: a cell counts only if at least half of it is cropland in WorldCover.
Why cells and not single fields. Plots in Jalna are about 1 to 2 hectares. At 10 to 60 m resolution we cannot see crop rows or individual small fields reliably. A 200 to 300 m cell averages many fields and is stable. This is why the product says "plots", not "fields", and never claims field-level diagnosis.

3. Coverage: what ground the data actually covers

4. Rainfall

NASA POWER gives modelled daily rainfall on a grid of about half a degree. We total June to October for 2018 to 2026. For 2026 the total was about 52% of the 2018 to 2025 average (our own calculation, consistent with the roughly 46% deficit reported for the region). Because the grid is coarse, two neighbouring talukas can share one rainfall cell. We therefore show rain as context, not a cause.

5. Farmland mask

ESA WorldCover classifies the ground at 10 m into classes such as tree cover, cropland, built-up and water. We keep a cell only if at least half of it is cropland. Limits: it is a 2021 map, so land use may have changed, and it says "cropland", not which crop. We assume the dominant Kharif crop in Jalna is soybean and cotton but do not map them separately.

6. How "normal" and the alert levels are built from this data

  • Crop-age alignment. Past seasons are slid in time so the green-up (NDVI crossing 0.30) lines up. A late-sown plot is then compared with other plots at the same crop age, not the same calendar date.
  • Normal for each cell and 10-day bin is built from the other seasons (never from the season being tested).
  • Levels: Watch when the gap below normal passes −0.18, Urgent past −0.36 (NDVI units, after alignment). These thresholds were tuned on seasons other than 2026 and then frozen. A plot with too little clear data is labelled Cannot see, never "fine".
  • Ranking: talukas are ranked by excess over the usual share of flagged farmland for that date, so a taluka that is always a bit high is not unfairly marked down.

What it does not show. There is no lead time: the signal first stands out 4 to 14 days after the order date (and in Bhokardan and Jafrabad was not detected before it). So the data shows where 2026 differs from ordinary seasons, not earlier than officials. And nothing has been confirmed on the ground yet.

8. Ground truth (planned, not collected)

We drew a blind, sealed field sample: 40 main plots and 12 spares, stratified across Partur, Ambad and Ghansawangi. The answer key is sealed and only its SHA-256 hash is committed, so we cannot adjust the sample after seeing results. No plot has been visited yet (0 observations). Field sheets and an evaluator are ready in validation/field/ and backend/evaluate_field.py.

9. Known limits of the data

  • 10 to 60 m resolution: we see fields turn from green to brown, not crop rows. A ripened or ploughed field can look like a dried one.
  • Cloud: mid-August pictures are only 20% to 40% clear in some areas, so the slider uses mid-September.
  • Rainfall is coarse; farmland mask is from 2021 and gives no crop type.
  • Eight seasons (2019 to 2026) is a short history for "normal".
  • The first Mantha box was placed with guessed coordinates and lay in Lonar, outside the district. We found this by checking every box against the outlines, moved it, and re-fetched. All earlier Mantha numbers were wrong and were replaced.
